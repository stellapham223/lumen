## 6. FAQ schema (8 items, Tier 2 hub)

**Q: How long is the menstrual phase?**
A: On a standard 28-day cycle, the menstrual phase is days 1 to 5. Bleeding lasting 3 to 7 days is within the typical range (ACOG guidance). Longer or shorter cycles scale roughly proportionally; the menstrual phase ends when bleeding stops, at which point the follicular phase technically continues.

**Q: What hormones are dominant during the menstrual phase?**
A: Both estrogen and progesterone are at their cycle minimum during the menstrual phase. Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) begins to rise in the second half of the phase, signaling the next follicle to develop. Prostaglandins (not technically hormones, but signaling lipids) are elevated locally in the uterus and drive contractions, which cause cramps.

**Q: Why am I so tired during my period?**
A: Three overlapping reasons: low estrogen reduces serotonin-supported energy and motivation; sleep quality dips on day 1 and 2 due to prostaglandin disturbance; and active iron loss (10 to 40 mg per cycle, per PubMed 16197581) can leave borderline-deficient women feeling fatigued. If fatigue is severe, ask your provider for a ferritin test, not just hemoglobin.

**Q: What should I eat during my menstrual phase?**
A: Prioritize heme iron sources (red meat, liver, sardines) at least once a day during bleeding. Plant iron sources (lentils, spinach, fortified grains) work too, but pair with vitamin C (citrus, berries, peppers) to double absorption. Separate coffee or tea from iron-rich meals by two hours. Magnesium-rich foods (dark chocolate, pumpkin seeds) may help cramp severity, though the evidence is weak.

**Q: Is it okay to work out during my period?**
A: Yes. Strength performance does not measurably drop during menstruation (McNulty et al. 2020 systematic review). Perceived exertion (how hard the work feels) does rise, so consider keeping intensity and reducing volume by 10 to 20 percent if your RPE is elevated. Low-intensity options (walking, yoga, mobility) are useful for symptom management.

**Q: What kind of work is the menstrual phase good for?**
A: Reflective, integrative, low-stimulus tasks: quarterly planning, OKR drafting, retrospectives, editing your own writing, learning new material, and 1:1s with trusted colleagues. The research on default-mode-network connectivity in low-hormone phases is consistent with this framing, though the effect size is small. Use the phase preference, not as a hard rule.

**Q: What should I avoid scheduling during the menstrual phase?**
A: When you have a choice, defer net-new sales pitches, high-stakes presentations, large-group networking, and confrontational conversations to weeks 2 or 3. When you do not have a choice, the workarounds are: more sleep the night before, NSAIDs at first cramp, and a written script for the high-stakes part (less reliance on in-the-moment confidence).

**Q: Is "right-brain dominance" during the menstrual phase real?**
A: It is overclaimed. The popular framing comes from one early book (FloLiving) and does not reflect the modern neuroimaging literature, which shows small and inconsistent left-right asymmetry shifts. What does replicate: lower whole-brain dynamical complexity in the early follicular and menstrual phases, which is consistent with reflective rather than performative cognition. Use this as a soft signal, not a rule.
