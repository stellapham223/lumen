## 6. FAQ schema (5-7 Q&A, will become FaqJsonLd)

**Q: Does cycle syncing actually work?**
A: It depends which protocol. A 2024 meta-analysis of 55 studies found no fitness benefit from phase-timed exercise. Symptom-aware scheduling (reducing cognitive load during late luteal when energy and focus predictably drop) is mechanistically plausible but under-studied. Phase-based diet rules and supplement cycling lack quality trials. The honest answer is to grade each protocol separately rather than treat cycle syncing as one yes-or-no question.

**Q: Is cycle syncing scientifically proven?**
A: No, not as a complete prescriptive system. Hormonal fluctuations across the cycle are well-documented and do affect cognition, mood, and physical capacity in measurable ways. But the specific protocols popularized as cycle syncing (eat this in luteal, do barre on day 16, supplement cycle by phase) lack randomized controlled trial evidence. The physiology is real; the prescriptive layer is largely inference.

**Q: Is cycle syncing a fad?**
A: The TikTok version with strict food rules and phase-specific workouts is closer to a fad. The underlying observation, that a 28-day hormonal cycle affects energy and cognition, is grounded physiology. The fad part is the leap from real biology to specific lifestyle prescriptions that have not been tested. Treat the prescriptions as hypotheses, not protocols.

**Q: What does the science actually say about cycle syncing?**
A: Hormonal effects on cognition and mood are documented in clinical literature (estrogen-serotonin link, progesterone-GABA, BDNF and neuroplasticity in the follicular phase). Effect sizes are smaller than wellness headlines suggest. Group-level studies miss large individual variation, so the strongest scientific case is for tracking your own pattern over 2-3 cycles before committing to any protocol.

**Q: Why do so many women say cycle syncing works for them?**
A: Three reasons. First, paying attention to your cycle is itself useful information, regardless of the specific intervention. Second, anything that reduces stress or improves sleep will improve PMS symptoms; cycle syncing routines often bundle in those benefits. Third, expectation effects are real. None of this means cycle syncing is fake, but it means the active ingredient may not be the phase-specific prescription.

**Q: What part of cycle syncing has the best evidence?**
A: Symptom-aware scheduling has the strongest mechanistic backing: hormone-cognition links are well-documented, and predictably reducing demands when you predictably crash (late luteal for many) has low downside. Phase-timed exercise has the worst evidence, with a 2024 meta-analysis finding no benefit. Diet rules and supplement cycling fall in between, with mostly null or low-quality trials.

**Q: Should I try cycle syncing if the evidence is weak?**
A: Symptom-aware scheduling is low-cost and worth a 2-cycle test if you have predictable energy or focus dips. Strict phase-timed workouts and supplement protocols are probably not worth the effort given the evidence. Track one symptom you want to change, run a structured 2-cycle test (one baseline, one intervention), and look for a clear shift rather than a vibe.
